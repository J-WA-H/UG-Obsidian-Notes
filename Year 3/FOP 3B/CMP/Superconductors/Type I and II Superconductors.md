For a type I semiconductor, the magnetisation as a function of the auxiliary field $H$ at a fixed temperature $T$  is
![[Pasted image 20250226181428.png]]
where until the critical field $H_{c}$, the gradient is the susceptibility of a [superconductor](Superconductivity.md), $\chi=-\frac{M}{H}=-1$. At $H_{c}$, the superconductor cannot repel the B-field as it requires too much work, so it easier for it to drop to the normal phase where magnetisation is zero.

For varying $T$, the critical $B$ field, $B_{c}=\mu_{0}H$ is
![[Pasted image 20250226181750.png]]
This has the form
$$B_{c}(T)=B_{c}(0)\left[ 1-\left( \frac{T}{T_{c}} \right)^2 \right].$$
This is the phase diagram for a type I superconductor - below the critical B-field is the superconducting phase and above it is the normal phase - where the work required to repel the B-field is larger than the energy for a phase transition.

This implies the superconductor has a critical current, the current generated by the critical B-field.
![[Pasted image 20250226182050.png]]
Consider a superconductor of diameter $2R$ and a B-field circling it.
From Ampere's law,
$$B(r)=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi r}$$
we get the critical current - $I_{max}$ - of 
$$B_{c}(T)=\frac{\mu_{0}I_{max}}{2\pi R}.$$
For type II superconductors, the magnetisation has two critical H fields, one where $M$ starts decaying, $H_{c_{1}}$ and one where $M=0$, $H_{c_{2}}$. The drop to zero magnetisation is no longer discontinuous.
![[Pasted image 20250226182427.png]]
For $H<H_{c_{1}}$, the material is in the superconducting phase. For $H_{c_{1}}<H<H_{c_{2}}$, the material is in the mixed/vortex state, and for $H>H_{c_{2}}$, the material is in the normal phase.
