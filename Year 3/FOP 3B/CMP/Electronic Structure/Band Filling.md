In a 1D array of atoms, the spacing between each point in k space is $\frac{2\pi}{L}$. The number of k points withing the first Brillouin zone is
$$\frac{\left( \frac{2\pi}{a} \right)}{\left( \frac{2\pi}{L} \right)}\times 2=\frac{2L}{a}$$
Since the number of atoms is given by $N=\frac{L}{a}$, this means there are $2N$ k points within the first Brillouin zone.
In 3D, the number of atoms moves to the number of unit cells.

For Group 1 elements, the outer band is half full so half of the energy states are full.
![[Pasted image 20250117160257.png]]
For group 2 elements, the outer band is full so all the states in the first Brillouin zone are full. However, this predicts Beryllium and Magnesium to be insulators, when they are observed to be conductors.

Consider a 2D solid
![[Pasted image 20250117160755.png]]
The [Fermi surface](Free-Electron%20Theory.md) doesn't entirely fill the first Brillouin zone, so some states leak into the second
![[Pasted image 20250117161021.png]]

For a large [band gap](Nearly-Free%20Electron%20Theory.md), it requires less energy for the states to fully occupy the first Brillouin zone than for it to fill up to the fermi surface leaking into the second, so the electrons in the 2nd BZ fully transfer to the first.
![[Pasted image 20250117161252.png]]

For small band gap, there is a partial transfer of electrons from the 2nd zone to the first, so the partially filled bands mean materials like this are good conductors. This is why Be and Mg are good conductors.
