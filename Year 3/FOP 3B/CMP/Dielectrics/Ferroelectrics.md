Ferroelectrics are strong [dielectrics](Electric%20Dipole.md).

Consider a $\text{BaTiO}_{3}$ compound, which has a cubic structure.
![[Pasted image 20250306114809.png]]
There is a small displacement of around $\thicksim 10^{-2} \mathring{\text{A}}$ of $\text{Ti}^{4+}$ due to the strain of the unit cell so there is a dipole moment $\mu$ in each unit cell.
This gives a spontaneous polarisation $\underline{P}_{s}$ without an E-field present. 

Consider a [polarised](Polarisation.md) ferroelectric.
![[Pasted image 20250306123000.png]]
From Maxwell I:
$$\underline{\nabla}\cdot \underline{D}=\rho_{f}=0$$
where $\rho_{f}$ is the free charge density.
$$\underline{\nabla}\cdot(\varepsilon_{0} \underline{E}+\underline{P})=0\implies \varepsilon_{0}\underline{\nabla}\cdot \underline{E}=-\underline{\nabla}\cdot \underline{P}.$$
Inside the solid, $P_{s}$ is large and outside, it is zero so there is a large gradient at the boundary.

For a single dipole, the energy $E=-\underline{\mu}\cdot \underline{E}$. The energy density is then
$$\varepsilon=-\underline{P}\cdot \underline{E}.$$
The lowest energy configuration is given by domains.
![[Pasted image 20250306123839.png]]
There is no $\underline{P}_{s}$ gradient so no E-field gradient so this is the lowest energy.
Like for [Ferromagnets](Domain%20Wall%20Energy.md), this creates a hysteresis loop.
