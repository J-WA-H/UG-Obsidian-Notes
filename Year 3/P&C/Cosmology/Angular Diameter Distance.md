Consider a sphere in Euclidean space centred on an observer
![[Pasted image 20250304134822.png]]
Consider an object of length $l$ at a distance $d$ from the observer. The observer sees the object with angular diameter $\Theta$. These are related by
$$l=d\Theta \implies d_{A}=\frac{l}{\Theta}.$$
$d_{A}$ is the angular diameter distance. 

In a universe described by the [[Friedmann-Robertson-Walker Metric]], a theoretical value for $d_A$ can be found.
$$ds^2=-c^2dt^2+a^2[dr^2+S_{k}^2(r)(d\theta^2+\sin ^2\theta d\phi^2)].$$
Consider the separation of 2 events, $A$, $B$, where each event describes the emission of light from either end of the object.
As both events lie on the surface of the sphere, they are the same distance from the observer, $dr=0$ so the time emitted is the same for $A$ and $B$, $dt=0$. They also lie at the same line of latitude so $d\phi=0$, leaving only $d\theta=\Theta$.
The separation of these two events is then
$$ds^2=a^2S_{k}^2(r)\Theta^2$$
$$\therefore l=ds=aS_{k}(r)\Theta \implies \frac{l}{\Theta}=aS_{k}(r)$$
This gives a theoretical value for the angular diameter distance,
$$d_{A}=\frac{S_{k}(r)}{1+z}.$$
Comparing this with the [luminosity distance](Flux-Luminosity%20Relation.md),
$$d_{L}=(1+z_{e})S_{k}(r)=(1+z)^2d_{A}$$
This emphasises that distance measurements depend on the method.

Perturbations in a medium create ripples that travel away from the perturbation centres, at a speed determined by the internal properties of the medium - this can be used as a standard ruler.

Consider the emission of light from two ends of an object,
![[Pasted image 20250304142203.png]]
As the universe expands, the observer moves further away so it take longer for the light waves to reach the observer. However, $\Theta$ remains constant in time so $d_A$ is the physical distance at the time of emission.

This distance is very sensitive to $k$. For two light beams emitted parallel to each other.
- In a flat universe, $k=0$, the light beams remains parallel
- In a closed universe, $k>0$, the light beams converge
- In an open universe, $k<0$, the light beams diverge.
![[Pasted image 20250304142956.png]]

Consider a closed universe,
$$d_{A}=\frac{S_{k}(r)}{1+z}=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{ k }}\sin(\sqrt{ k }r)}{1+z}< \frac{r}{1+z}$$
so for a closed universe, the distance measured is smaller than a flat universe. The reverse is true for an open universe.
