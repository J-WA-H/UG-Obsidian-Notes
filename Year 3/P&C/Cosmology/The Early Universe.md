The early universe was in the [radiation dominated era](Standard%20Cosmological%20Model.md). The radiation density is
$$\rho_{R}c^2=\frac{4\sigma T_{\gamma}^4}{c}.$$
This includes relativistic neutrinos, $\rho_{\nu}\approx 0.68\rho_{\gamma}.$

When the universe reached a high enough temperature, where $k_{B}T>mc^2$, the following process occurs to reach thermal equilibrium.
![[Pasted image 20250212141607.png]]
In the first branch, the high temperature means particles collide more frequently, which creates a uniform temperature - reaching thermal equilibrium.
In the second branch, the high temperature allows for particle - anti-particle production to occur, which is a reversible process, allowing particle production which gives thermal equilibrium.

The early universe had a plasma of elementary particle species, highly relativistic and in thermal equilibrium.

For the $i^{th}$ boson species, the density is given by
$$\rho_{b,i}c^2=\frac{g_{b,i}}{2} \frac{4\sigma T^4}{c}$$
where $g_{b,i}$ is the degeneracy of the energy state.

For fermions, 
$$\rho_{f,i}c^2=\frac{g_{f,i}}{2} \frac{7}{8} \frac{4\sigma T^4}{c}.$$
The factor of $\frac{7}{8}$ accounts for the difference between the [[Bose-Einstein Distribution]] and [[Fermi-Dirac Distribution]].

Accounting for all particle species,
$$\rho_{R}c^2=\sum_{i} \frac{g_{b,i}}{2} \frac{4\sigma T^4}{c}+\sum_{j} \frac{g_{f,j}}{2} \frac{7}{8} \frac{4\sigma T^4}{c}=\frac{1}{2}\left[ \sum_{bosons}g_{b,i}+\frac{7}{8}\sum_{fermions} g_{f,i} \right] \frac{4\sigma T^4}{c}.$$
The bracket is known as $g_{*}$, the effective bosonic degrees of freedom.

$g_{*}$ changes through time as matter content changes.

![[Pasted image 20250212143046.png]]

The photon era is around $0.8\text{ eV}<k_{B}T<0.5\text{ MeV}$, where it is dominated by photons and neutrinos.
$$g_{*}=[2]_{\gamma}+\frac{7}{8}[6]_{\nu} \left( \frac{4}{11} \right)^{4/3}.$$
The photon has two polarisations, so $g_{\gamma}=2$, and there are 3 neutrinos, and 3 anti-neutrinos so $g_{\nu}=6$.

To consider $e^{-}/e^{+}$ annihilation, the degrees of freedom for this has to be considered, $g_{e}=2\cdot 2=4$. where there are two particle types, the electrons and positrons, and 2 spins.
$$g_{*}=2+\frac{7}{8}\cdot 6 +\frac{7}{8}\cdot 4\approx 10.75.$$
The photon era transitions into the lepton era as $e^{+}/e^{-}$ dominates $g_*$.

When $k_{B}T\approx 1\text{ MeV}$, neutrino decoupling happens. This is where neutrinos stop interacting with other particles, so $T_{\nu}\neq T_{\gamma}$.
$$\rho_{\gamma}c^2=\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2} \frac{4\sigma T^4_{\gamma}}{c};\;\rho_{\nu}c^2=\frac{g_{\nu}}{2} \frac{4\sigma T^4_{\nu}}{c}$$
$$\implies \left( \frac{T_{\nu}}{T_{\gamma}} \right)=\left( \frac{4}{11} \right)^{1/3}.$$
$$\therefore \rho_{\nu}c^2=\frac{g_{\nu}}{c} \frac{4\sigma T^4_{\gamma}}{c}\left( \frac{T_{\nu}}{T_{\gamma}} \right)^4=\frac{g_{\nu}}{2} \frac{4\sigma T_{\gamma}^{4}}{c}\left( \frac{4}{11} \right)^{4/3}.$$
The factor of $\left( \frac{4}{11} \right)^{4/3}$ is absorbed into $g_\nu$, which explains this factor in $g_{*}$.
$$\rho_{R}c^2=\frac{1}{2}\left[ g_{\gamma}+g_{\nu}\left( \frac{4}{11} \right)^{4/3} \right] \frac{4\sigma T_{\gamma}^4}{c}.$$

For $200\text{ MeV}<k_{B}T<10^{19}\text{ GeV}$, the universe was in the quark era.
At the lower bound, the quark-hadron phase transition is believed to occur. Below this energy, quarks are bound into baryons, anti-baryons, and mesons of all energetically possible variants.
Above this energy, the universe is full of free quarks, leptons, and gluons.

Towards $k_{B}T\approx 100\text{ GeV}$, the electroweak phase transition happens, and the electromagnetic and weak forces unify into a single force. Above this energy, the $W^{+}$ and $Z^{0}$ bosons become massless like photons, and the Higgs boson. Meanwhile, the relativistic top quarks and their anti-quarks are now produced in abundance which contributes $\frac{7}{8}\cdot 12$ to $g_*$. This gives the maximal value of $g_{*}$ form the standard model.

For $k_{B}T>10^{15}\text{ GeV}$, a grand unified theory is needed to explain the quark and lepton interactions as weak, strong and EM forces are unified. It is likely that the GUT model would introduce a large number of new particles, further increasing $g_{*}$, but these have not yet been found.

The Planck era is $k_{B}T>10^{19}\text{ GeV}$. In this era, gravitational interactions operate at the same strength as the other fundamental forces. A quantum theory of gravity is needed to describe this, which has not yet been established so the detailed behaviour of the universe in this stage cannot be calculated.
