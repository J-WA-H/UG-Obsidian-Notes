The radial velocity method measures the line-of-sight reflex motion of the star around a star-planet barycentre. For a star of mass $M_{*}$ orbiting a planet of mass $M_{p}$, the observed stellar reflex velocity amplitude is
$$K=\left( \frac{2\pi G}{T} \right)^{1/3} \frac{M_{p}\sin i}{(M_{p}+M_{*})^{2/3}} \frac{1}{(1-e^2)^{1/2}}.$$
Circular orbits lead to sinusoidal variations and if $M_{p}\ll M_{*}$ then
$$K=28.4 \left( \frac{T}{1\text{ year}} \right)^{-1/3}\left( \frac{M_{p}\sin i}{M_{J}} \right)\left( \frac{M_{*}}{M_{\odot}} \right)^{-2/3}\text{ ms}^{-1}.$$
The astrometric method measures positions on the sky with $\mu as$ accuracy.

$$\alpha=\frac{M_{p}}{M_{p}+M_{*}} \frac{a}{d}\approx \frac{M_{p}}{M_{*}} \frac{a}{d}.$$
The astrometric method is more sensitive for long-period planets, so compliments the radial velocity method. This is also applicable for hot or rapidly rotating stars, unlike the radial method.

If the semi-major axis is known from spectroscopy, distance from the stars from parallax and stellar mass from spectral type, then $\alpha$ yields the planets mass with no dependence on orbital inclination.
