Another method used in [[Detecting Exoplanets]] is the transit method.
We need a favourable orbital inclination to observe a transit. If a uniform brightness star and planet all in the stellar disc then the fractional luminosity dip during the transit is given by
$$\frac{\Delta L}{L_{*}}=\left( \frac{R_{p}}{R_{*}} \right)^2.$$
Knowing the stellar radius we can calculate planetary radius from the transit density.

Derivation of transit duration:
![[Pasted image 20241127124130.png]]
$d$ is the distance travelled during transit.
$$d=2(R_{*}+R_{p})\cos\delta.$$
For a circular orbit of radius $a$, $d$ represents a fraction of the orbit
$$f=\frac{d}{2\pi a}=\frac{2(R_{*}+R_{p})\cos \delta}{2\pi a}.$$
Hence, a transit last for the same fraction of the time period
$$\tau=\frac{T}{\pi}\left( \frac{{R_{*}+R_{p}}}{a} \right)\cos\delta=13\left( \frac{M_{*}}{M_{\odot}} \right)^{-1/2}\left( \frac{a}{1\text{ Au}} \right)^{1/2}\left( \frac{R_{*}}{R_{\odot}} \right)\text{  hours}.$$
$\delta$ is the latitude of the transit.

Knowing everything else, we can infer $\delta$ and hence inclination
$$\cos i=\frac{R_{*}\sin\delta}{a}.$$
The probability of a randomly oriented orbit giving transit is
$$p=\frac{R_{*}}{a}=\cos i_{min}.$$
