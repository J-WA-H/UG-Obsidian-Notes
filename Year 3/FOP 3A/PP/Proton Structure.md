To determine the structure of a proton, the [structure function](Structure%20Functions.md) is experientially examined from [[Inelastic Scattering]] at high energies $Q^2\approx 100\text{ GeV}$, varying $X$.
![[Pasted image 20250130190754.png]]
The ideal model is a $\delta$ function at $X=\frac{1}{3}$ which suggests 3 point charges in a proton. The particles that make up a proton are partons.

In this situation, the proton consists of three particles held together by the strong force, as it needs a binding force much stronger than the electromagnetic to overcome repulsion.
![[Pasted image 20250130191347.png]]
The structure function can be modelled by summing over the constituent particles,
$$\frac{\partial^2\sigma}{\partial Q^2\partial X}=\sum_{c}f_{c}(X)\left( \frac{\partial^2\sigma}{\partial Q^2\partial X} \right)_{c}$$
where $f_{c}(X)$ is the particle distribution function (PDF), the probability of hitting constituent $c$.

The scattering is electromagnetic as an electron was used as the bullet so $f_{c}=0$ for anything without charge as it does not interact using the electromagnetic force.

For some $Q^2$ we have
$$F(X)=X\sum_{c} Q_{c}^2f_{c}(X)$$
where $Q_c$ is the charge of the constituent particle.

Experimentally the structure function differs from this ideal case so to fix $f_{c}$ to experiment we need more than 3 point charges.

Inside the proton we have a lot of binding energy and Heisenberg uncertainty. It turns out that protons contain more than 3 quarks.

To understand this examine the separation of quarks.
![[Pasted image 20250130192416.png]]
As two quarks are stretched, the force between them grows stronger until a $q\bar{q}$ pair is formed. This means quarks cannot exist in isolation This $q\bar{q}$ pair is a meson.

Consider the PDFs of the individual quark types in a proton,
![[Pasted image 20250131094639.png]]
As these are not $\delta$-functions these are not the only particles in the proton. Considering the stretching between quarks, one possible structure of the proton could be 
![[Pasted image 20250131095258.png]]
where an $u\bar{u}$ pair is formed between two of the quarks.
So far the low $X$ parts of the PDFs have been omitted. In reality it actually looks like.
![[Pasted image 20250131095456.png]]
In measurements at low $X$ we see $u\bar{u}$, $d\bar{d}$, $s\bar{s}$, $c\bar{c}$ pairs inside the proton in addition to the $uud$ quarks.

In the total the proton contains 3 valence quarks, $uud$, and an unknown number of sea quarks, $q\bar{q}$ pairs. The number of sea quarks depends on $X$.
This takes from the simplistic model to the realistic model.
![[Pasted image 20250131095804.png]]
As $Q^2$ increases, there is more momentum transferred so more sea quarks.

For deep inelastic scattering we either measure sea quarks (Heisenberg), excitations $(\Delta^{+})$ or the proton is split up.

In summary, protons are more complicated than three quarks. Scattering gives particle distribution functions. Initially only two types of quarks but for high $Q^2$, low $X$ gives more. Sea quarks do not change the charge of the particle.

The proton is believed to be the only stable particle.
