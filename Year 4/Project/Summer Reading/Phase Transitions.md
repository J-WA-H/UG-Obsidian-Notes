Phase transitions in the universe are analogous to transitions in CMP. At high temperatures, spontaneously broken symmetries can be restored. The expectation of the Higgs field can be thought of as a Bose condensate of Higgs particles. at non-zero temperature, there is a thermal distribution of various particles and antiparticles. The particle masses are determined by the Higgs expectation value and, from this, the free energy of the system,
$$F=E-TS,\tag{5.1} $$
is a non-trivial function of $\phi$. The equilibrium value of $\phi$ is found by minimising the free energy and is generally temperature dependent. If the Higgs field becomes smaller, the particle masses decreases, so the available phase space becomes larger so the entropy increases. From this the Higgs field tends to decrease as the temperature increases and above some critical temperature, $T_{c}$, vanishes completely. $T_{c}$ is of the order of $\eta$.

In the Hot Big Bang model, the universe starts at a very high temperature so in the initial equilibrium value of the Higgs field is zero. As the universe expands and cools, it undergoes a phase transition at $T=T_{c}$, when the symmetry is spontaneously broken. A grand unified model can predict a series of symmetry breakings so there are a series of phase transitions, for example
$$G\to H\to\dots\to SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\to SU(3)\times U(1)_{em}.\tag{5.2} $$
To describe symmetry restoration, it was shown that the free energy density is given by the same expansion as the [[Effective Potential]] with all the Green's functions replaced by finite temperature Green's functions. This gives the finite temperature effective temperature,
$$V_{eff}(\phi,T)=\frac{F(\phi,T)}{\mathcal{V}}\tag{5.3} $$
where $\mathcal{V}$ is the volume. The one-loop correction is
$$V_{eff}(\phi,T)=V(\phi)+\sum_{n}F_{n}(\phi,T)\tag{5.4} $$where
$$F_{n}=\pm \int \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\ln\left( 1\mp \exp\left( -\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{T} \right) \right).\tag{5.5}$$
Evaluating this gives the high temperature effective potential
$$V_{eff}(\phi,T)=V(\phi)+\frac{{\lambda+3e^2}}{12}T^2|\phi|^2-\frac{2\pi^2}{45}T^4.\tag{5.6} $$
Like in CMP, there are first and second order cosmological phase transitions. For a second order transition, the high temperature effective potential is
$$V_{eff}(\phi,T)=m^2(T)|\phi|^2+\frac{\lambda}{4}|\phi|^4\tag{5.7} $$where
$$m^2(T)=\frac{\lambda}{12}(T^2-6\eta^2)\tag{5.8} $$
is the effective mass squared of the field in the symmetric state $\langle \phi \rangle=0$. The critical temperature is when the effective mass is zero, $T_{c}=\sqrt{ 6 }\eta$. For $T>T_{c}$, $m^2(T)$ is positive so the minimum of $V_{eff}$ is at $\phi=0$ so the expectation of the field vanishes and symmetry is restored. For $T<T_{c}$, $m^2(T)$ is negative so the symmetric state becomes unstable and $\phi$ develops a non-zero expectation value. Minimising $V_{eff}$ for $T<T_{c}$ gives
$$|\phi|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{ 6 }}(T_{c}^2-T^2)^{1/2}.\tag{5.9} $$
This is a second order transition as the order parameter $|\phi|$ grows continuously from zero as the temperature is decreased below the critical temperature.

Cosmologically, when the universe cools the field develops a non-zero magnitude however the phase of $\phi$, $\theta$, is also dependent on random fluctuations, and can vary in different regions in space. Minimising the free energy means that the spatial variations in $\theta$ gradually die out, with the length scale at which this happens defined by $\xi(t)$, which obeys the causality bound $\xi(t)<d_{H}(t)$.
The critical temperature is comparable to $\eta$ but in general there is some dependence on coupling constants. In the Higgs model it is
$$T_{c}=\left( \frac{6\lambda}{\lambda+3e^2} \right)^{1/2}\eta.\tag{5.10} $$
For $T<T_{c}$, the scalar field develops an expectation value corresponding to some point in the manifold $\mathcal{M}$ of the minima of $V_{eff}$. Since all points are equivalent, the choice will be given by random fluctuations in $\phi$, which forms topological defects.

For a first order transition, the Higgs model has a finite-temperature effective potential is
$$V_{eff}(\phi,T)=V_{eff}(\phi,0)+\frac{3T}{2\pi^2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkk^2\ln\left(1-e^{ -\sqrt{ k^2+2e^2|\phi|^2 }/T }\right).\tag{5.11} $$
For $|\phi|\ll \frac{T}{e}$ we get
$$V_{eff}(\phi,T)=m^2(T)|\phi|^2+\frac{3e^4}{16\pi^2}|\phi|^4\ln \left( \frac{|\phi|^2}{\sigma^2} \right).\tag{5.12} $$
At high temperatures, $V_{eff}$ is dominated by the quadratic term and has a single minimum at $\phi=0$. When the symmetry breaking minimum first occurs, it is not thermodynamic favourable but below some critical temperature, it becomes deeper than the symmetric minimum so there is a discontinuous jump in scalar potential so this is a first order transition.
