The [Nambu action](Actions.md) (15.7) gives an accurate description of string motion provided that the string does not intersect itself or another string. The zero-width approximation breaks down near the crossing point and so to determine the ultimate outcome of a collision the underlying non-linear field theory must be studied. Strings cannot pass through each other but it is possible to have a topological obstruction to [[Reconnection]]. In this case, the strings become entangled, with perhaps a third string stretching between them. These strings evolve very differently from those which reconnect and are more difficult to resolve with the standard cosmology.

Every string intersection between locally straight segments can be completely characterised by two collision parameters, the angle $\theta$ subtended by the strings and a relative perpendicular velocity $v$. This is easy to demonstrate for two string solutions $\vec{x},\vec{y}$ in the transverse gauge, [(16.9)](Strings%20in%20Flat%20Spacetime.md), with tangent vectors $\vec{x}',\vec{y}'$ and velocities $\dot{\vec{x}},\dot{\vec{y}}$. Choose a frame $\Sigma$ in which the first string is stationary and lies along the $x$-axis, $\dot{\vec{x}}=0,\vec{x}'=\hat{e}_{1}$ (near the crossing point). Now, $\vec{x}(\zeta,t)$ will remain invariant under longitudinal Lorentz boosts in the $x$ direction and also under arbitrary rotations about the $x$-axis. A boost can be employed to set $\dot{{y}}_{1}=0$, and then a rotation to set $\dot{{y}}_{2}=0$ and $y_{3}'=0$ (since $\dot{\vec{y}}\cdot \vec{y}'=0$). Thus for the second string we obtain
$$\tag{18.1} \begin{matrix}
\dot{\vec{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
0 \\
v
\end{bmatrix} \\
\vec{y}'=(1-v^2)^{1/2}\begin{bmatrix}
\cos \theta \\
\sin \theta \\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{matrix}$$
This corresponds to string intersection in the $xy$-plane with a $z$-directed relative velocity $v$.
At every intercommuting, pieces of string that were previously moving independently become connected. On the opposite sides of the point of intersection the newly connected string will will point in different directions and will be moving with different velocities. That is, at the moment of intersection $t_{0}$, $\vec{x}'(\zeta_{0},t_{0})$ and $\dot{\vec{x}}(\zeta_{0},t_{0})$ change very rapidly as functions of $\zeta$ on a length scale comparable to the string thickness. This region of rapid variation is called a kink and, in the zero-width approximation, $\vec{x}'$ and $\dot{\vec{x}}$ are discontinuous at the kink. This implies that the functions $\vec{a}(\zeta-t)$ and $\vec{b}(\zeta+t)$ describing the motion of the string after intercommuting must have discontinuous derivatives at certain values of their arguments. The kink, therefore, does not disappear after intercommuting. Instead, it splits into two kinks running along the string in opposite directions. A single kink is present in the special case when only one of the functions $\vec{a}'$ and $\vec{b}'$ is discontinuous. The kink due to a discontinuity in $\vec{a}'$ has a fixed value of $\zeta-t$, with a velocity given by
$$\frac{d\vec{x}}{dt}=\frac{1}{2}\vec{b}'\left( 1+\frac{d\zeta}{dt} \right)=\vec{b}'\tag{18.2} .$$
Since $|\vec{b}'|=1$, the kink must move at the speed of light. Similarly, the kink due to a discontinuity in $\vec{b}'$ remains at a fixed value of $\zeta+t$ and has a velocity $\dot{\vec{x}}=-\vec{a}'$ with $|\dot{\vec{x}}|=1$. The angle subtended by a single kink - its opening angle - in any frame must be greater than $\frac{\pi}{2}$. The sharpest kinks are created when nearly antiparallel strings reconnect, where the resulting double line separates into two right-angled kinks.

Closed loops can self-intersect and break into smaller loops. One could try to describe loop fragmentation as a stochastic process in which the parent loop fragments into two daughters with some probability $p$. Each of the daughters has the same probability of further fragmentation and so on, with the fragmentation probability remaining unchanged in the subsequent generations. For $p< \frac{1}{2}$ this model predicts that the end result of loop fragmentation is a finite set of non-interacting loops, while for $p> \frac{1}{2}$ the average number of fragments is infinite. Consider the fragmentations of loops described by
$$\begin{matrix}
a(\zeta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\cos(n\zeta+\varphi_{n}), \\
b(\zeta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{n}\cos(n\zeta+\varphi_{n}),
\end{matrix}\tag{18.3} $$
where $N=10$ and the phases $\varphi_{n}$ are chosen at random between $0$ and $2\pi$. The invariant length of the loop $L$ is then normalised to $2\pi$. Consider two types of loops where $a_{n},b_{n}$ were randomly chosen between $(i)$ 0 and 1 and $(ii)$ between 0 and $\frac{1}{n^2}$. Type (i) loops were very convoluted while type (ii) loops were relatively smooth, with higher harmonics supressed. Almost all the initial loops were self-intersecting so according to the naïve stochastic model one would expect an infinite number of loops in the final state. However, the fraction of self-intersecting loops became smaller in each subsequent generation, and after several generations the fragmentation process stopped completely. For both types, the characteristic size of the largest fragment was comparable to the typical curvature radius of the parent loop. The average velocity of the fragments is $v\sim{0}.6$, with smaller fragments typically having a larger velocity.

The failure of the stochastic model is not very surprising if we note that loop fragmentation is a completely deterministic process. The condition that a loop described by the functions $\vec{a}(\zeta-t)$ and $\vec{b}(\zeta+t)$ self-intersects is that
$$\vec{a}(\zeta_{1}-t_{0})+\vec{b}(\zeta_{1}+t_{0})=\vec{a}(\zeta_{2}-t_{0})+\vec{b}(\zeta_{2}+t_{0})\tag{18.4} $$for some values of $\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}>\zeta_{1}$ and $t_{0}$. At the moment of intersection, the daughter loop, corresponding to the points $\zeta_{1}\leq \zeta\leq \zeta_{2}$, is described by the same functions $\vec{a},\vec{b}$ as the parent loop. The invariant length of the daughter loop is $\ell=\zeta_{2}-\zeta_{1}$ and we can use the closure condition $(16.16)$ to continue these functions beyond the range $\zeta_{1}<\zeta<\zeta_{2}$:
$$\begin{matrix}
\vec{a}(\zeta_{-}+\ell)=\vec{a}(\zeta_{-})-\vec{\Delta}, \\
\vec{b}(\zeta_{+}+\ell)=\vec{b}(\zeta_{+})+\vec{\Delta}.
\end{matrix}\tag{18.5} $$Here $\zeta_{\pm}=\zeta\pm t$ and $\vec{\Delta}$ is a constant vector determined by the intersection points on the original loop,
$$\vec{\Delta}=\vec{b}(\zeta_{2}+t_{0})-\vec{b}(\zeta_{1}+t_{0})=\vec{a}(\zeta_{1}-t_{0})-\vec{a}(\zeta_{2}-t_{0}).\tag{18.6} $$
The new functions $\vec{a}(\zeta_{-})$ and $\vec{b}(\zeta_{+})$ have discontinuous derivatives at $\zeta_{-}=\zeta_{1}-t_{0}$ and $\zeta_{+}=\zeta_{1}+t_{0}$, respectively, indicating that the daughter loop acquires a pair of kinks propagating in the opposite directions. The second daughter loop, which has the invariant length $L-\ell$, can be treated in the same manner.
In general, $(18.4)$ can have several solutions for potential intersection points of the parent loop. Since the daughter loops are described by the same functions $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, they can inherit some of these intersections. However, some of the intersections can be lost if the corresponding points on the string belong to different daughters. New intersection points can also be created, since $(18.4)$ can acquire new solutions due to the changed periodicity properties of the functions $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$. Viewed in this way, the fragmentation process is a competition between the creation of new intersection points and the destruction of old ones. The stochastic model can apply only if the number of intersection points per loop remains roughly constant throughout the fragmentation process. Since the number of loops is sharply increased in each generation, this would require that the creation of intersection points to be much mire efficient than their destruction, also numerical simulations suggest that this is not the case.
This observation becomes clearer by considering loops consisting only of straight segments separated by kinks. Since every kink subtends an angle greater than $\frac{\pi}{2}$, each closed loop must process at least 5 kinks. By following a single fragment from a parent loop with $n$ kinks, we see that at each splitting a daughter loop must lose at least 5 kinks to its sibling, while gaining only two from the intersection - a net loss of $\geq 3$ kinks. This loss of structure can only continue for at most $\frac{n}{3}$ generations, and will end when all the daughter loops have achieved relatively simple configurations with only a few kinks.
Finally, we comment on an inheritance law for cusps. Cusps on a parent loop correspond to intersections of the curves $\vec{a}'(\zeta_{-})$ and $-\vec{b}'(\zeta_{+})$ on the unit sphere. If the loop has no kinks and is not very convoluted, so that the typical curvature radius of the curves is $\sim 1$, then in general the curves have $2m$ intersections, where $m$ is a small integer. Daughter loops inherit parts of the $\vec{a}'$ and $\vec{b}'$ curves of the parent loop and it is easily seen that no new cusps can be created during the fragmentation process. On the other hand, old cusps can be destroyed if the intersecting parts of the $\vec{a}'$ and $-\vec{b}'$ curves belong to different daughters. Hence the total number of cusps in the daughter loops cannot exceed the number of cusps in the parent loop.
